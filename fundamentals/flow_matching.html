Flow Matching for TTS — Minimal PyTorch

Flow Matching Generative Models

The Entire Idea

Learn a velocity field that transports noise to speech along a straight line. At inference, start from noise and follow the velocity for N steps.

t=0 (noise) t=1 (clean mel) · ─────── velocity ──────────────▶ · x₀ = randn x₁ = mel spectrogram Path: x_t = (1-t) · noise + t · mel (straight line) Velocity: u = mel - noise (constant!) Model learns: v_θ(x_t, t) ≈ u

Key Terms Demystified

What is x_t?

The noisy intermediate sample at timestep t — a point along the straight line from noise to speech.

t=0 t=0.3 t=0.7 t=1 pure noise mostly noise mostly speech clean mel x₀ x₀.₃ x₀.₇ x₁ x_t = (1-t) · noise + t · mel t=0: 1.0 · noise + 0.0 · mel = pure noise t=0.3: 0.7 · noise + 0.3 · mel = mostly noise, faint speech t=0.7: 0.3 · noise + 0.7 · mel = mostly speech, faint noise t=1: 0.0 · noise + 1.0 · mel = clean mel spectrogram

During training: you sample a random t, construct x_t, and ask the model "given this partially noisy mel, predict the velocity toward clean speech." mel is given — it's the ground truth from your dataset.

During inference: you don't have mel (that's what you're generating). So you start at x₀ (pure noise) and take small steps following the model's predicted velocity until you arrive at x₁ (clean mel).

What is the velocity field?

The direction and magnitude to move x_t toward clean speech at each point. Think of it like wind on a map:

At every point in the space, there's an arrow saying: "if you're here, move THIS direction to get closer to speech" t=0 t=0.5 t=1 noise ──→ ──→ ──→ ──→ speech v v v v (all arrows point the same way because the path is straight)

For flow matching, the velocity is trivially simple: velocity = mel - noise. It doesn't change with t. The "wind" blows in the same direction the whole time.

The model's job is to learn this velocity from data. At inference you don't know mel - noise (you don't have mel), so the model predicts it.

What is OT-CFM? (It's just MSE)

The fancy name breaks down as:

WordWhat it means
Optimal TransportUse straight-line paths (linear interpolation) — the shortest route from noise to speech
ConditionalThe flow is conditioned on a specific data sample (this particular mel)
Flow MatchingRegress a neural net's velocity prediction toward the true velocity

The entire loss: loss = MSE(model(x_t, t, text), mel - noise). That's it. Standard supervised regression.

Training — 5 Lines of Logic (Zero Steps!)

Training uses zero steps. There is no walking, no ODE, no loop. It's one forward pass per sample, like any standard supervised learning.

# mel: (B, 80, T)  — ground truth mel spectrogram (GIVEN from dataset)
# text: (B, ...)   — text conditioning (phonemes, characters, etc.)

t     = torch.rand(B, 1, 1)                # random timestep ~ U(0,1)
noise = torch.randn_like(mel)               # start point: pure Gaussian noise
x_t   = (1 - t) * noise + t * mel          # interpolate noise → speech
target = mel - noise                        # ground truth velocity (constant!)
loss  = F.mse_loss(model(x_t, t, text), target)  # regress predicted → true velocity

That's the OT-CFM loss — and it's literally just MSE. The "optimal transport" part means we use straight-line paths. The model sees a random (x_t, t) pair and learns "at this noisiness level, the velocity should be this." Over millions of samples, it sees every t from 0 to 1 and learns the velocity everywhere. But it never sequentially walks from 0 to 1 during training.

Inference — 4 Lines of Logic

The number of inference steps is completely independent of training. You choose N_steps freely at deployment based on your latency budget. The same trained model works with any step count:

# ODE solve: walk from noise to speech in N_steps
# N_steps = 32 (slow, accurate) or 4 (fast, still decent) or 1 (fastest)

x = torch.randn(1, 80, T)                  # start from pure noise
for i in range(N_steps):
    t = torch.tensor(i / N_steps)
    x = x + (1 / N_steps) * model(x, t, text)  # Euler step

# x is now a mel spectrogram → feed to vocoder for waveform

9 total lines. Everything else — DiT architecture, speaker conditioning, text encoder, vocoder — is what goes inside model() and what you do after. The flow matching part is just these 9 lines.

What is an Euler Solver?

The simplest method to solve an ODE — "I know my current position and the direction to move, so I take a small step in that direction."

# What you have:   position x, velocity v, step size dt
# What you do:     x_new = x + dt * v
# That's Euler.

Same as walking with a compass:

You're at position A. Compass says "go northeast." You walk 10 meters northeast. Now you're at B. Check compass again. "Go east." Walk 10 meters east. Now you're at C. ...repeat until you arrive. More steps = more compass checks = more accurate: 10 steps (dt = 0.1): ·→·→·→·→·→·→·→·→·→· accurate 4 steps (dt = 0.25): ·──→·──→·──→·──→· pretty good 1 step (dt = 1.0): ·────────────────→· rough

Fancier solvers (Midpoint, RK4) take multiple compass readings within each step to be more accurate. But Euler is the baseline and what most flow matching TTS uses.

Why is the Path Straight?

Because you defined it that way. It's a design choice, not a consequence of the solver.

# Straight (flow matching — YOUR design choice):
x_t = (1 - t) * noise + t * mel          # linear interpolation

# Curved (diffusion — their design choice):
x_t = sqrt(α_t) * mel + sqrt(1 - α_t) * noise   # nonlinear in t

The curved version needs more steps to follow accurately. The straight version is easy — that's the whole advantage.

Euler is how you walk the path at inference. The straightness comes from how you defined the path during training. Two completely separate concepts.

Can 1 step work?

In theory, yes. If the model perfectly learned the constant velocity mel - noise, one Euler step lands exactly on the target.

In practice, no. The model's prediction is approximate (finite capacity, finite data). With 1 step, that small error is your final answer. With more steps, each step corrects the drift.

1 step: ·───────────→ · (one shot, small error, no correction) ↕ small gap from target 4 steps: ·──→·──→·──→·→· (re-check direction each step, self-correcting) ≈ target

That's why real TTS uses 4-10 steps — not because the math needs it, but because the model isn't perfect.

AD-FlowTSE gets away with 1 step because speaker extraction is easier — the answer is already in the input. TTS generates from scratch, so model error is larger and you need more steps to compensate.

Why This Works

The path is a straight line

x_t = (1-t) · noise + t · mel is linear interpolation. The velocity along this line is mel - noise — a constant that doesn't depend on t. So in theory, one Euler step would be exact if the model perfectly learned the velocity.

In practice, the model's prediction isn't perfect, so you take multiple steps to correct accumulated error. But the straighter the path, the fewer steps you need.

Diffusion vs flow matching — the path shapes

Diffusion (SDE): Flow Matching (ODE): noise · noise · \ \ \ curved, \ straight, \ stochastic \ deterministic \ \ · speech · speech 20-50 steps needed 6-10 steps (1-4 with distillation)

Full Working Example — Toy Flow Matching

A complete, runnable script that trains flow matching on random data. Replace the dummy data with real mel spectrograms + text features for actual TTS.

import torch
import torch.nn as nn
import torch.nn.functional as F

# ── Minimal velocity network ──────────────────────────────
# In real TTS: this is a DiT or UNet conditioned on text + speaker.
# Here: a small MLP that takes (x_t, t) and predicts velocity.

class VelocityNet(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, dim=80, hidden=256):
        super().__init__()
        self.net = nn.Sequential(
            nn.Linear(dim + 1, hidden),   # +1 for timestep t
            nn.GELU(),
            nn.Linear(hidden, hidden),
            nn.GELU(),
            nn.Linear(hidden, dim),
        )

    def forward(self, x_t, t):
        # x_t: (B, 80)  t: (B, 1)
        return self.net(torch.cat([x_t, t], dim=-1))

# ── Training ──────────────────────────────────────────────

model = VelocityNet()
opt = torch.optim.Adam(model.parameters(), lr=1e-3)

for step in range(5000):
    mel = torch.randn(64, 80)          # (B, 80) — fake target mel frames

    # === Flow matching core (5 lines) ===
    t     = torch.rand(64, 1)           # (B, 1)
    noise = torch.randn_like(mel)        # (B, 80)
    x_t   = (1 - t) * noise + t * mel    # interpolate
    target = mel - noise                  # true velocity
    loss  = F.mse_loss(model(x_t, t), target)

    opt.zero_grad()
    loss.backward()
    opt.step()

    if step % 1000 == 0:
        print(f"step {step}  loss {loss.item():.4f}")

# ── Inference ─────────────────────────────────────────────

model.eval()
N_steps = 10

x = torch.randn(1, 80)               # start from noise
for i in range(N_steps):
    t = torch.tensor([[i / N_steps]])
    with torch.no_grad():
        x = x + (1 / N_steps) * model(x, t)

print(f"Generated shape: {x.shape}")   # (1, 80) — one mel frame
# In real TTS: x would be (1, 80, T) — full mel spectrogram

The flow matching logic doesn't change. The 9 lines stay the same. All complexity lives in the model architecture and conditioning — not in the generative framework itself.

Variant: AD-FlowTSE (Speaker Extraction)

The AD-FlowTSE paper (ICASSP 2026) modifies flow matching for target speaker extraction. The key changes:

# Standard flow matching (TTS): noise → speech
x_t    = (1 - t) * noise + t * mel
target = mel - noise

# AD-FlowTSE: background → target speech
x_τ    = (1 - τ) * background + τ * target_speech   # τ = mixing ratio, not time
target = target_speech - background               # still constant!

The trick: the mixture x = τ·s₁ + (1-τ)·b already sits on the trajectory at position τ. So at inference, estimate τ̂, skip to that point, and integrate only [τ̂, 1].

Result: one step is exact (the path is linear, the velocity is constant).

This trick does NOT transfer to TTS. In TTS, the input is text/noise — it doesn't contain the target speech. There's no mixing ratio to exploit. For TTS, fewer steps come from consistency distillation, not from input-adaptive shortcuts.

Progressive Distillation — Reducing Inference Steps

The base flow matching model is trained with zero steps (random t snapshots). Distillation is a separate, second training phase that compresses the inference procedure.

Phase 1: Train base model (no steps, MSE on random t) → works well at 32 inference steps
Phase 2: Distillation (separate training run) → train student to do it in fewer steps

The idea: teacher takes 2 steps, student matches in 1

Teacher (32 steps): ·→·→·→·→·→·→ ... →· (many small steps) ╰─╯ ╰─╯ 2 steps 2 steps Student (16 steps): ·──→·──→· ... ──→· (fewer, bigger steps) 1 step 1 step Then repeat: Round 1: Teacher 32 steps → Student 16 steps Round 2: Teacher 16 steps → Student 8 steps Round 3: Teacher 8 steps → Student 4 steps Round 4: Teacher 4 steps → Student 2 steps

Minimal code — 6 lines of core logic

# teacher: already trained flow matching model (frozen)
# student: fresh copy (trainable)
# Goal: student does in 16 steps what teacher does in 32

teacher.eval()
student = copy.deepcopy(teacher)
opt = torch.optim.Adam(student.parameters(), lr=1e-4)

N_teacher = 32                            # teacher uses 32 steps
N_student = 16                            # student learns to use 16
dt_teacher = 1 / N_teacher                # small step size
dt_student = 1 / N_student                # big step size (2x)

for step in range(5000):
    mel   = torch.randn(64, 80)          # ground truth from dataset
    noise = torch.randn_like(mel)

    # Pick random starting point on the student's timestep grid
    k = torch.randint(0, N_student, (64, 1))
    t = k.float() / N_student
    x_t = (1 - t) * noise + t * mel      # construct x_t

    # === Core: teacher 2 steps, student 1 step ===
    with torch.no_grad():
        v1 = teacher(x_t, t)
        x_mid = x_t + dt_teacher * v1       # teacher step 1 → halfway
        v2 = teacher(x_mid, t + dt_teacher)
        x_target = x_mid + dt_teacher * v2  # teacher step 2 → target

    v_student = student(x_t, t)
    x_pred = x_t + dt_student * v_student  # student: 1 big step

    loss = F.mse_loss(x_pred, x_target)   # match teacher's 2 steps in 1

    opt.zero_grad()
    loss.backward()
    opt.step()

# Now student does in 16 steps what teacher did in 32.
# Repeat: student becomes new teacher, train 16→8→4→2

The core is 6 lines: teacher takes 2 small steps → x_target. Student takes 1 big step → x_pred. Loss = MSE(x_pred, x_target). The student learns to make one accurate jump wherever teacher needs two compass checks.

The cost: 4 total training stages to reach 4 steps

Stage 1: Base flow matching training → 32-step model Stage 2: Distill 32 → 16 → 16-step model (student becomes teacher) Stage 3: Distill 16 → 8 → 8-step model (student becomes teacher) Stage 4: Distill 8 → 4 → 4-step model (deploy this one)

4 separate training runs. Each time the student becomes the next teacher. That's the main downside — it's expensive and tedious.

Interview talking point: "Progressive distillation is the safe bet — simple MSE loss, reliable results, but costs 4 separate training runs to get from 32 to 4 steps. Consistency distillation is more aggressive — one training run, can reach single-step, but the loss is harder to tune. Pick based on your engineering budget."

Why Step Reduction > Quantization for Flow Matching

OptimizationSpeedupHow
INT8 quantization~1.5-2xCheaper per-step compute
Step reduction (32 → 4)~8xFewer forward passes
Both combined~12-16xOrthogonal — multiply the gains

Each ODE step = one full forward pass through the DiT/UNet. Cutting steps is a linear speedup. No quantization method can match going from 32 to 4 steps.

Best strategy: distill steps first (32→4), then quantize the remaining 4 forward passes.

The Full Picture: 3 Phases

Phase 1: Base training (zero steps) Random t snapshots → learn velocity field → MSE loss Result: model that works at 32 inference steps ↓ Phase 2: Progressive distillation (separate training) Teacher 2 steps → Student 1 step → repeat halving Result: model that works at 4 inference steps ↓ Phase 3: Quantization (your expertise) INT8 the remaining 4 forward passes → 1.5-2x on top Result: 4 steps × INT8 = ~12-16x total speedup Each phase is independent. Different training runs. Different optimizations.
