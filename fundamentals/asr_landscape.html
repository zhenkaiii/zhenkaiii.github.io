The acoustic model does the hard work. The LM rescorer is cheap insurance — it catches errors like "recognize speech" vs "wreck a nice beach" by scoring which hypothesis is more likely. Because it only sees K short strings (not raw audio), it's fast even on CPU.

This two-box model is your anchor. Everything below is about what changed inside each box — and one paradigm that merges them.

Inside the acoustic model: what "decoder" actually means

Your acoustic model isn't one monolithic pass. It has three sub-components, and the "decoder" people talk about is hiding inside it:

The key insight: Encoder and prediction network run independently — one processes audio, the other processes text history. The joint network combines them at each step to decide: emit a token or emit "blank" (= advance to next audio frame). Beam search explores multiple paths through the (time t, text step u) grid to produce top-K hypotheses.

When people say "decoder" in ASR, they mean the prediction network + joint network — the part that turns encoder representations into text. It was always inside your acoustic model box. You just never needed to name it separately.

CTC vs Transducer: the clean distinction

Both are loss functions / decoding algorithms, not models. "Beam search" is also an algorithm, not a model. The model is the encoder (+ optionally the prediction network + joint network). The loss determines how you train it:

CTCTransducer
ArchitectureEncoder onlyEncoder + Prediction Net + Joint Net
LossCTC lossTransducer (RNN-T) loss
Language modelingNone — each frame independentYes — prediction network conditions on previous tokens
Needs LM rescorer?Yes, essentialOptional

CTC as a standalone production system is mostly obsolete — transducer does everything CTC does plus output-dependency modeling. But CTC survives as an auxiliary training loss: many modern transducer systems add total_loss = transducer_loss + 0.3 * ctc_loss because the CTC term forces the encoder to learn good frame-level alignments on its own, without leaning on the prediction network.

Whisper (2022): the power shift from acoustic model to language model

In traditional ASR, the acoustic model is king. You spend years engineering better encoders, better frame-level features, better alignment losses. If the acoustic model gets "cat" vs "cut" wrong, the LM can't save you.

Whisper flipped this. The encoder is a vanilla Transformer — nothing fancy, no Conformer, no specialized speech tricks. Instead, all the capacity goes into a full Transformer decoder that acts as a powerful language model. The loss is plain cross-entropy (predict next text token, same as GPT) — no CTC loss, no transducer loss, nothing speech-specific. The secret weapon: 680K hours of labeled data.

Whisper didn't solve acoustic modeling better. It made acoustic modeling less important by giving the decoder enough capacity and data to compensate. If the encoder is uncertain about "cat" vs "cut," the decoder knows "the cat sat on the mat" is far more likely. Same pattern as everywhere in AI: specialized architecture replaced by scale + simple architecture + data.

Why Whisper can skip the rescorer

The trade-off: Whisper's decoder is more powerful because it sees everything at once, but that's exactly why it can't stream. RNN-T's predictor is weaker but works frame-by-frame, enabling real-time output.

What changed since Alexa era: three shifts

Shift 1: The encoder got better (RNN/LSTM → Conformer)

In the Alexa era, the acoustic model encoder was typically LSTM layers. Around 2020, Google introduced the Conformer — a hybrid block that sandwiches self-attention between convolutions:

# Conformer block = sandwich of four components:
x = x + FFN(x)          # feed-forward
x = x + MHSA(x)         # multi-head self-attention (global context)
x = x + Conv(x)         # depthwise convolution (local phonetic patterns)
x = x + FFN(x)          # feed-forward again

# Why this matters:
#   LSTM only sees the past (unidirectional) or is slow (bidirectional)
#   Attention captures long-range dependencies efficiently
#   Convolution captures local structure (formant transitions, phoneme boundaries)
#   Together → better than either alone for speech

The Conformer is now the default encoder in nearly every modern ASR system. Your mental model stays the same — just replace "LSTM encoder" with "Conformer encoder" inside the acoustic model box.

Shift 2: Self-supervised pre-training (wav2vec 2.0, 2020)

Before: you needed thousands of hours of labeled audio (transcribed speech) to train an acoustic model. Expensive.

wav2vec 2.0 (Meta) showed you could pre-train the encoder on unlabeled audio (just raw speech, no transcripts) and then fine-tune with a small CTC head on labeled data. This is the "BERT moment" for speech — same idea as masked language modeling, but masking audio frames instead of words.

Impact: dramatically reduced the labeled data requirement. Languages with little transcribed data (low-resource) suddenly became feasible.

The current landscape: three paradigms

Now map these shifts back to your two-box model. Today there are three paradigms — they differ in how much of the LM rescorer they absorb and where the "intelligence" lives:

1. CTC — encoder only, needs your rescorer

Audio → Conformer encoder → frame-level logits (T × vocab) → CTC decode → collapse repeats, remove blanks → text

Encoder only, trained with CTC loss. Each frame is predicted independently — no language modeling. Your LM rescorer is essential.

As a standalone system, mostly obsolete — transducers do everything CTC does plus output-dependency modeling. CTC's modern role is as an auxiliary training loss that helps the encoder learn better frame-level alignments, even when the final system uses transducer decoding.

Pros: Fastest inference (no decoder at all), simplest to implement.

Cons: Frame independence → "recognize speech" vs "wreck a nice beach" errors without external LM.

2. Transducer (RNN-T) — your Alexa system, evolved

Audio → Conformer encoder → encoded frames + Prediction network (text history → embedding, like a mini LM) → Joint network → next token probability

This is the direct descendant of Alexa-era ASR. The key evolution: the transducer's prediction network is a small built-in LM that conditions each output on the previous tokens. So the acoustic model box now has a mini language model inside it.

The external LM rescorer becomes optional. The prediction network handles most language-level corrections. You can still add a rescorer for extra accuracy, but many production systems skip it.

ModelDetail
Conformer-TransducerGoogle's on-device ASR. Powers Google Assistant, Pixel phones. This is the successor to your Alexa system.
Zipformer (k2/icefall)Efficient variant with varied attention spans per layer. State-of-the-art open-source streaming.
FastConformer (NVIDIA)Optimized Conformer with subsampling. NeMo framework.

Pros: True streaming (emit tokens as audio arrives), on-device friendly, built-in language modeling.

Cons: More complex training (RNN-T loss), slightly lower accuracy than Whisper on long-form audio.

Relation to your model: the prediction network absorbed some of the rescorer's job. Your two-box diagram is becoming 1.5 boxes.

3. Encoder-Decoder (Whisper) — the power shifted to the decoder

Audio → Transformer encoder → encoded features (T × d) → Transformer decoder (autoregressive) → text tokens one at a time

Vanilla Transformer encoder (not the star) + full Transformer decoder (the star). Trained with cross-entropy loss — same loss as GPT, nothing speech-specific. No CTC, no transducer loss. The decoder is the language model. Your two boxes are now one.

The encoder still matters (without it the decoder just hallucinates fluent but wrong text — a known Whisper failure mode on silence). But the balance of power shifted: the decoder's language model compensates for encoder uncertainty.

Whisper pseudocode

# Whisper — trained with cross-entropy loss, same as GPT
mel = log_mel_spectrogram(audio, n_mels=80, hop=160)  # (80, T)

encoded = transformer_encoder(mel)    # vanilla Transformer, not Conformer
# encoded: (T/2, d_model)            # /2 from conv downsampling

# Decoder IS the language model — generates text autoregressively
tokens = []
for step in range(max_len):
    logits = transformer_decoder(tokens, encoded)  # cross-attn to audio
    next_token = argmax(logits[-1])                # + self-attn over text
    tokens.append(next_token)
    if next_token == EOT: break

text = tokenizer.decode(tokens)       # BPE → string

Frame size vs chunk size: two different things

Don't conflate these — they operate at different levels:

Mel spectrogram frame = ~10ms (not a design choice)

The mel spectrogram is computed with a ~25ms window and ~10ms hop, producing one 80-dim vector every 10ms. This is a universal standard because speech changes on a 10-50ms timescale — a phoneme like "t" can be as short as 20ms. If you made frames 400ms, you'd blur multiple phonemes into one frame and the encoder couldn't distinguish "cat" from "cap."

Streaming chunk = how many frames to buffer before running the encoder (design choice)

In streaming mode, the encoder doesn't run on every single 10ms frame. It waits for a chunk of frames, processes them together, then waits for the next chunk. This is a pure latency vs accuracy tradeoff:

Chunk sizeFrames bufferedLatency addedEncoder contextUse case
40ms4 framesVery lowTiny — less accurateGame voice chat (must feel instant)
160ms16 framesModerateGood tradeoffVoice assistants (Alexa, Google)
400ms40 frames0.4s delayMore context — more accurateLive captions, subtitles
Full utteranceAll framesWait until doneFull context — best accuracyWhisper (offline transcription)

Bigger chunk = encoder sees more context per forward pass = better predictions, but the user waits longer. Whisper is the extreme: chunk size = entire utterance. Best accuracy, worst latency.

Far-field ASR: solved before the acoustic model

When the speaker is far from the microphone — think smart speakers, game consoles, conference rooms — the signal is weak, reverberant (echoes off walls), and mixed with background noise or other speakers. This is not solved by a better acoustic model. It's an engineering problem resolved by a front-end signal processing pipeline that cleans the audio before it reaches your ASR system.

Critical requirement: multi-channel audio. You need a microphone array (e.g. Alexa Echo has 7 mics). A single microphone cannot do beamforming — you need multiple channels to exploit spatial information about where the sound is coming from.

Key point: the acoustic model itself doesn't change for far-field. You add a front-end before it. Each stage handles a different problem:

StageProblem it solvesRequires multi-channel?
BeamformingSuppress sound from other directionsYes — needs mic array for spatial filtering
Speech enhancementRemove residual noise + reverberationNo — works on single-channel too
Speaker targeting"Which person do I transcribe?"No — uses speaker embeddings, not spatial info
Noise-robust trainingMake acoustic model more tolerantNo — data augmentation (add noise/reverb to training data)

Whisper is unusually noise-robust without a front-end because it trained on 680K hours of messy real-world audio (YouTube with background music, poor recordings). But it still can't handle multi-speaker far-field without a front-end — it doesn't know which speaker to transcribe.

Interview answer: ASR (30 seconds)

"Classic ASR has two stages: an acoustic model — the heavy-duty part that maps audio frames to text hypotheses — and a lightweight LM rescorer that picks the best one. The field evolved by gradually absorbing the LM into the acoustic model. With CTC, it's encoder-only trained with CTC loss — each frame is independent, so you still need the external rescorer. Transducers added a prediction network inside the acoustic model that acts as a mini LM, making the rescorer optional — this is today's standard for streaming. Then Whisper flipped the balance of power entirely: vanilla Transformer encoder, full Transformer decoder, trained with plain cross-entropy — no CTC loss, no transducer loss. The decoder is the language model. It can't stream, but for offline transcription it's the most robust option. For real-time — games, voice assistants — you still want a Conformer-Transducer."
