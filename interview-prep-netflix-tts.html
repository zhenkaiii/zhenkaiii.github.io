1. If Asked: "What do you know about TTS?" ~60 sec

Modern TTS has converged on three paradigms. First, autoregressive codec language models like VALL-E treat speech as discrete audio tokens and generate them with a transformer — same architecture as GPT, just predicting audio codes instead of text. Great prosody and zero-shot voice cloning, but slow because tokens are generated sequentially.

Second, flow-matching models like F5-TTS generate a mel spectrogram by iteratively denoising noise, conditioned on text and a speaker embedding. Highest quality, non-autoregressive, but you need 6-32 ODE steps plus a vocoder.

Third, single-pass feed-forward models like VITS with a built-in vocoder — one forward pass, ~30ms, but no zero-shot voice cloning.

The 2026 trend is hybrid: an AR language model generates coarse semantic tokens (prosody, rhythm), then a flow-matching model fills in the fine acoustics. CosyVoice from Alibaba is the canonical example.

End with: "The part I'd contribute to is making these models fast enough for on-device or real-time — that's where quantization, step distillation, and streaming come in." This pivots back to your strength.

2. Q&A Bank

Paradigm Questions

What's the difference between AR codec TTS and flow-matching TTS?

AR codec LM (VALL-E, CosyVoice stage 1): generates discrete tokens one at a time, like GPT. A neural audio codec (e.g., EnCodec) compresses waveforms into integer sequences. The transformer predicts these integers autoregressively. Then the codec decoder converts them back to audio.

Flow-matching (F5-TTS, VoiceBox): generates continuous mel spectrograms by solving an ODE that transports noise to speech. Non-autoregressive — the full spectrogram is generated in parallel across time. Then a vocoder (HiFi-GAN, BigVGAN) converts mel to waveform.

Key tradeoff: AR gives better prosody (it models temporal dependencies explicitly), flow-matching gives better acoustic quality and is faster per-utterance.

Why is the field moving toward flow-matching over diffusion?

Diffusion uses a stochastic differential equation (SDE) — it adds and removes Gaussian noise. Flow-matching uses an ordinary differential equation (ODE) — deterministic trajectories.

ODE trajectories are straighter, so you need fewer integration steps to reach the target. Diffusion needs 20-50 steps for quality; flow-matching gets comparable quality in 6-10 steps, and with consistency distillation you can push to 1-4 steps.

In practice, most "diffusion TTS" papers in 2025-2026 are actually flow-matching under the hood.

What is VITS and when would you use it?

VITS is a single-pass, end-to-end TTS model. It combines a VAE, normalizing flows, and a built-in HiFi-GAN decoder — text goes in, waveform comes out in one forward pass, ~30ms.

The catch: no zero-shot voice cloning. Speakers must be in the training set (lookup table). So you use VITS when latency is king and the speaker set is fixed — e.g., a specific NPC voice in a game, or a known set of narrators.

How does zero-shot voice cloning work?

Three mechanisms depending on paradigm:

1. In-context learning (VALL-E): encode a 3-sec reference clip into audio tokens, prepend as prompt. The LM "continues" in that voice — same as giving GPT a few-shot example.

2. Speaker embedding conditioning (F5-TTS, CosyVoice): extract a speaker embedding (a single ~192-dim vector from a speaker verification network like ECAPA-TDNN), inject it as a condition to the generator.

3. Speech infilling (F5-TTS specifically): concatenate reference audio + blank region, and the model fills in the blank matching the reference voice. Elegant — no explicit speaker encoder needed.

Efficiency & On-Device Questions (Your Strength)

How would you make TTS faster for real-time / on-device?

Five levers, in order of impact:

1. Step reduction: for flow-matching, distill 32 steps down to 1-4 steps via consistency distillation or progressive distillation. This alone is a 5-8x speedup — hard to match with quantization alone.

2. Quantization: INT8 is safe for TTS (I've done this for ASR). INT4 is risky — audio is sensitive to quantization noise in the vocoder. You'd want mixed-precision: INT4 for the text encoder, INT8 for the acoustic model and vocoder.

3. Streaming / chunked generation: don't wait for the full utterance. Generate mel in overlapping chunks (e.g., 500ms each), start the vocoder on the first chunk while generating the next. Reduces time-to-first-audio.

4. Architecture choice: for strict latency budgets, VITS-style single-pass models (~30ms) beat any flow-matching setup.

5. Runtime optimization: ONNX Runtime, Core ML, or TensorRT for GPU. Operator fusion, KV-cache optimization for the AR stage.

Why is step reduction more impactful than quantization for flow-matching TTS?

Quantization gives you ~1.5-2x speedup (INT8) at best. Step reduction gives you 8-16x (32 steps → 2-4 steps). The math is simple: each ODE step is a full forward pass through a DiT or UNet. Cutting steps cuts the number of forward passes linearly.

Of course, the best approach is both — distill steps first, then quantize the remaining forward passes. They're orthogonal optimizations.

What's the full latency breakdown for a speech agent (ASR → LLM → TTS)?

Architecture Deep-Dive Questions

What's the semantic / acoustic hierarchy in modern TTS?

The field has converged on two-level generation:

Semantic layer: prosody, rhythm, phoneme timing. "What to say and how." Generated by an AR language model (slow, sequential, but captures long-range dependencies).

Acoustic layer: timbre, texture, waveform detail. "Exactly how it sounds." Generated by flow-matching or codec decoder (parallel, fast).

Examples: CosyVoice uses Qwen2.5-0.5B for semantic tokens → flow-matching for mel. VALL-E uses AR for RVQ layer 0 → NAR for layers 1-7.

This separation works because semantics depend on meaning (needs language model), while acoustics depend on speaker identity (needs conditioning, not reasoning).

What's F5-TTS and why is it considered elegant?

F5-TTS frames TTS as a speech infilling task. You concatenate a reference audio clip with a blank region, and the model fills in the blank conditioned on text.

Why it's elegant — it removes three components other systems need: no G2P (works on raw characters), no duration model (filler-token padding lets the model learn alignment implicitly), and uses a lightweight ConvNeXt text encoder instead of a Transformer.

Inference: DiT (Diffusion Transformer) with an ODE solver, default 32 steps. With Sway Sampling (non-uniform timesteps biased toward early denoising), can reduce to 6-8 steps.

What's a vocoder and why is it needed?

A vocoder converts a mel spectrogram (2D: frequency × time, ~80-100 bins) into a raw waveform (1D: audio samples at 24kHz). It's needed because mel is a lossy, low-dimensional representation — you lose phase information, and you need to reconstruct ~320 audio samples per mel frame.

Common vocoders: HiFi-GAN (transposed convolution upsampling, GAN-trained), BigVGAN (larger, higher quality), Vocos (used by F5-TTS). VITS doesn't need a separate vocoder because its decoder IS a HiFi-GAN built into the model.

Flow Matching & Generative Models

Can you explain flow matching in simple terms?

You define a straight-line path from noise to speech: x_t = (1-t) · noise + t · mel. That's just linear interpolation. The velocity along this line is mel - noise — a constant that doesn't change with t. Think of it as wind on a map: at every point, an arrow says "move this direction to get closer to speech." All arrows point the same way because the path is straight.

Training (zero steps — no walking): sample a random t, construct x_t, and train the model to predict the velocity via MSE. That's it. The mel spectrogram is given from the dataset — standard supervised learning, one forward pass per sample.

Inference (N steps — you choose freely): start from noise, use the model's predicted velocity to take small steps toward speech. This is an Euler solver — "I know my position and the predicted direction, so I step forward." More steps = more accurate, but the same trained model works with any step count.

The training loss is called OT-CFM (optimal transport conditional flow matching). Sounds fancy — it's literally just MSE between predicted velocity and true velocity.

Why is the path straight? How is that ensured?

It's straight by definition — you chose it when you wrote x_t = (1-t) · noise + t · mel. That's linear interpolation. Nothing "ensures" it; it's your design choice.

Diffusion uses a curved path: x_t = sqrt(α_t) · mel + sqrt(1-α_t) · noise — nonlinear in t. Curved paths need more steps to follow accurately. Straight paths are easy.

The Euler solver is how you walk the path at inference. The straightness comes from how you defined training pairs. Two separate concepts.

Can flow matching do 1-step inference?

In theory, yes — the true velocity is constant, so one Euler step is mathematically exact.

In practice, no — the model's prediction is approximate (finite capacity, finite data). With 1 step, that small error is your final answer. With more steps, each step re-checks the direction and corrects drift from the previous step.

That's why real TTS uses 4-10 steps: not because the math needs it, but because the model isn't perfect. AD-FlowTSE gets away with 1 step because extraction is easier — the answer is already in the input. TTS generates from scratch, so model error is larger.

How many steps does training use?

Zero. Training has no loop, no ODE, no walking. It's one forward pass per sample: pick random t, construct x_t, predict velocity, compute MSE, backprop. The model sees random (x_t, t) snapshots across millions of training iterations and learns the velocity at every t. It never sequentially walks from 0 to 1.

Only inference walks the path. And the number of inference steps is a pure deployment-time choice — independent of anything in training.

What about that ICASSP 2026 paper on adaptive flow matching — AD-FlowTSE?

That's a colleague's work on target speaker extraction (not TTS, but related generative speech). The key idea: instead of flowing from noise → speech, they flow from background → target speech, and replace the diffusion time variable with the physical mixing ratio τ.

Since a mixture is literally τ · target + (1-τ) · background, the mixture already sits at a known point on the trajectory. So you estimate τ, skip to that point, and only integrate the remaining [τ, 1] interval. The vector field simplifies to a constant (s₁ - b), meaning the trajectory is perfectly linear — one Euler step is exact.

Result: single-step inference that beats multi-step baselines. More steps actually hurt because they accumulate numerical error on an already-linear path.

Does that one-step trick transfer to TTS?

No, not directly. The trick works for extraction because the input already contains the target — you just need to subtract the background. The trajectory is linear by construction.

In TTS, you're synthesizing speech from scratch. The input is text (or noise), not a mixture containing the answer. There's no physical mixing ratio to exploit, and the noise → speech trajectory is not linear.

For TTS, the route to fewer steps is consistency distillation or rectified flow — training the model to produce good outputs in fewer steps, rather than exploiting input structure.

The transferable principle is: adapt compute to input difficulty. Easy inputs should cost less. That applies everywhere — early exit networks, adaptive quantization, speculative decoding.

Netflix-Specific Questions

Where does TTS fit in Netflix's stack?

Likely use cases: automated dubbing (translate content into other languages with synthetic voices that match the original actor's timbre), audio descriptions (narrate visual content for accessibility), interactive content (game-like experiences with dynamic NPC dialogue), and previews / trailers (generate voiceovers at scale).

The hard problem for Netflix specifically is voice consistency across hours of content — a character must sound the same in episode 1 and episode 10. Zero-shot cloning from a 3-sec clip isn't enough; you'd want speaker-specific LoRA fine-tuning for main characters.

If you were building Netflix's TTS pipeline, what architecture?

Depends on the use case:

Dubbing (offline, quality is king): hybrid AR + flow-matching (CosyVoice-style). AR LM for prosody that matches the original performance, flow-matching for acoustic quality. Run offline with 10-32 ODE steps. LoRA fine-tuned per character.

Interactive / real-time (latency is king): VITS or distilled flow-matching (1-4 steps) with streaming vocoder. Pre-defined speaker set. Target: <200ms time-to-first-audio.

Audio descriptions (one narrator, moderate quality): flow-matching with 4-8 steps. Fine-tune on the narrator voice. Doesn't need to be real-time but should be efficient for batch processing.

Pivot-Back Questions

How does your quantization experience apply to TTS?

Directly. The vocoder (HiFi-GAN, BigVGAN) is the most latency-sensitive component and benefits enormously from INT8 quantization — it's essentially a stack of transposed convolutions, same operator profile as the encoder/decoder in ASR.

The challenge is that audio is perceptually sensitive to quantization noise. INT4 often introduces audible artifacts in the vocoder. My approach at Alexa was quantization-aware training with learned centroids (Lloyd-Max), which preserves quality at low bit-widths. That technique applies directly to TTS vocoders.

For the flow-matching backbone (DiT/UNet), INT8 is typically fine because it's generating mel spectrograms, not raw waveforms — the vocoder smooths out minor quantization errors.

What's harder to quantize in TTS vs. ASR?

The vocoder. In ASR, the output is text (discrete) — small numerical errors don't matter as long as the argmax doesn't change. In TTS, the output is a continuous waveform — small errors are directly audible as distortion, buzzing, or metallic artifacts.

Additionally, TTS has the generative model (diffusion/flow) where quantization errors compound across steps. Each ODE step feeds into the next, so a small error at step 1 grows by step 10. That's why fewer steps + higher precision often beats many steps + lower precision.
